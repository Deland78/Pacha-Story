# The Comet of Revelation (1532 CE)

*"The stones remember what the living forget."*
— Attributed to Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui

---

"He should see it."

His grandson's voice, somewhere above him. Hands sliding beneath his shoulders, his legs. Nayra felt himself lifted—light now, so light—and carried through the doorway into cold night air.

They set him down on blankets against the stone wall. His son knelt on one side, his grandson on the other. Above them, the sky.

The comet blazed.

Brighter than any star, its tail streaked across the darkness like a wound torn in the fabric of Hanan Pacha. Nayra stared. His family stared. Somewhere in the village, a woman began to weep.

"Pachakuti," his grandson whispered.

Yes. Nayra knew it. But this comet—this comet was wrong. Different. A stranger.

The cold bit into his skin. His eyes slid closed.

**His mother's arms around him, small body trembling. Four years old. A comet overhead, tail sweeping the southern sky.**

**"Your father watches with the ancestors now," she said. "The star carries him."**

**He had not understood. He understood now.**

A tremor ran through him. His son's hand tightened on his shoulder.

**Last year. Standing outside this same house, neck craned back. Another comet, familiar somehow, an old friend returning. The empire already cracking—Atahualpa and Huáscar tearing it between them. The priests had whispered: the cycle turns. Pachakuti approaches.**

His breath rattled. The comet's light pressed against his closed lids.

**Tiwanaku, but not in ruins. Whole. Alive. Firelight glowing from a thousand dwellings, the Akapana rising dark against the stars. On the hills above the city, a young man stood alone, watching the same comet cross the sky. Nayra did not know his name, but he knew him—felt him—across the centuries. The comet's tail reflected in the man's eyes, and something else: a flicker of flames that were not there, a city burning that had not yet burned. The young man shuddered, wrapped his arms around himself, turned back toward the lights below. He would tell no one what he saw.**

Someone wiped his brow. The cloth came away damp.

**A city of lights. Sprawling to every horizon, glittering like stars fallen to earth and spreading without end. Two figures on a rooftop—a man and woman, young, holding hands. They strained to see the comet through the glow, the ancient signal dimmed by all those earthbound fires. Still, they were looking. Still reaching for what their ancestors had seen clearly.**

**The same comet. Cycling through centuries, visiting and revisiting—**

Nayra gasped. His eyes flew open. The comet above him now was not the same.

It was larger. Brighter. A stranger that had never walked these skies before. The familiar comet was a heartbeat, returning and returning. This comet was a blade, come once to cut.

His chest seized. The fever flared white-hot behind his eyes.

**Cusco burning.**

**He saw it as clearly as the comet overhead. The imperial city wreathed in flame. Temples consumed. Screams rising. Men in metal moving through the streets, but beside them—native faces. Allies of the strangers, turning against their own.**

**The burning city the young man on the hillside had seen five hundred years ago. The burning city that was always coming.**

The vision released him. Nayra lay gasping, the comet blazing above, the pattern humming through his failing body. Everything connected—he felt it now, even if he could not name each thread. The comets. The eclipses. His father's lost body. His own lost attention. His son's hand, pulling back.

His son's hand, now holding his.

"Father."

The word came from far away. Nayra turned his head. His son's face swam into focus—older than it should be, wet with tears.

"I am here," his son said.

Nayra's throat worked. The words came out broken, barely breath. "I was not. I was never—"

"I know."

"The horn. I was always listening for—"

"I know, Father."

Silence. The comet burned. His grandson stood a few paces back, watching.

"You came back," Nayra managed. "You stayed."

His son's grip tightened. "Someone had to."

The words cut and healed in the same stroke. Nayra felt tears slide down his temples, pooling in the hollows of his ears. He had no strength to wipe them.

"The eclipse," he whispered. "You reached for me."

His son went still.

"I saw it. I felt you reach. I did not—" A cough tore through him. "I am sorry."

His son said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly: "I stopped reaching."

"I know."

"I taught myself not to need—"

"I know." Nayra's fingers found his son's wrist, pressed weakly. "Teach yourself again. Reach again. Not for me. For him." His eyes moved to his grandson. "He will need you. What comes next—he will need someone who stays."

The comet blazed on. The stars watched.

His son leaned closer, voice rough. "What do you see, Father? In the visions—what is coming?"

Nayra looked at the strange comet, the blade that had come to cut. He thought of Cusco burning. Of the empire devouring itself. Of the stones that would outlast all of it.

"Fire," he said. "And after the fire—seeds."

His son did not understand. That was alright. Someday, someone would.

Somewhere in the village, a voice began the death song—small against the darkness, but rising.

Nayra closed his eyes. The pattern held him. The stones held him. His family's hands held him.

He let go.
