# The Son's Care (1532 CE)

*"When the Sun wishes to speak, he speaks through omens. When the people wish to hear, they must learn to see."*
— Attributed to Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui

---

Cool water touched his forehead. Nayra surfaced from fever-dark into the dim room, his son's face above him—older now, lines carved by years Nayra had watched but never truly seen.

"Be still." His son wrung the cloth into a clay bowl. "You were speaking to someone who wasn't here."

The water traced Nayra's temples, his cracked lips, the edges of the pustules that had spread to his neck. Gentle hands. Patient hands. His son lifted him to change the soaked blankets beneath, and Nayra felt himself light as a child, hollowed by sickness.

The cloth returned. Cool. Steady.

*This is what I never gave.*

The thought arrived without warning, sharp beneath the fever's blur. He had been there for his son's childhood illnesses—sitting in the corner, one ear turned toward the door. He had been present at meals—chewing slowly, listening for the pututu's distant call. Always in the room. Never in the moment. The horn could sound any time, day or night, and he would have to run. So he was always already running, even while sitting still.

Now his son's hands moved with a tenderness Nayra had never taught him. Where had he learned it? Not from his father.

---

*They were eating together—Nayra, his wife, their son. Twelve years old, the boy, asking something about the river, about fish, about going together to—*

*Nayra held up his hand. Listened.*

*Nothing. Wind. A bird. No horn.*

*"What were you saying?"*

*His son's face had closed. "Nothing."*

*"You were asking about the river."*

*"It doesn't matter."*

*His wife gave him a look he had learned not to meet. Nayra returned to his food, but his attention had already scattered—calculating whether the morning's message from Hatunqolla had reached Cuzco yet, whether the relay had held through the night, whether the next call would come before dawn.*

*When he looked up again, his son had left the room.*

---

"Grandfather?"

His grandson's voice pulled him forward. The young man crouched in the doorway, a cup steaming in his hands.

"Coca tea. It will ease the pain." He brought it to Nayra's lips, and the bitter warmth slid down his throat. His son stepped back, making room.

"There is news," his grandson said, glancing between them. "Atahualpa has defeated Huáscar. The war is decided."

Nayra's son exhaled slowly. They all knew what this meant. Tiwanaku had declared for Huáscar. The new Sapa Inca would remember.

"We will send tribute," his son said. "Declare our loyalty. Perhaps—"

But Nayra had stopped listening. The coca spread through him, loosening something, and the room began to tilt.

**His mother's arms around him. Small body pressed against her warmth. He was four years old, and she was telling him his father would not return.**

**"He fell fighting the Sapa Inca's armies in the eastern valleys. They could not bring his body home."**

**Above them, a light streaked across the night sky—a star with a tail, brighter than any star should be. His mother held him tighter.**

**"The ancestors are watching," she whispered. "They see everything."**

**No burial. No wrapping of the body. His father wandered somewhere between worlds, never to become a proper ancestor. And Nayra would grow up shaped by that hollowness—learning to hold himself apart, to keep one ear always turned toward a door, toward a duty, toward anything but the person in front of him—**

**The vision shuddered, leapt—**

**He stood beside his son on the hillside above the village. The boy was twelve. They had walked here together, a rare afternoon when Nayra had not been called.**

**The sun began to vanish.**

**A ring of fire in the sky—the sun eaten from within, leaving only a blazing edge. And below it, where Lake Titicaca should have spread silver and vast, there was only white. Salt flat. Cracked earth. The water gone as if it had never existed.**

**His son made a sound—half gasp, half sob. The boy's hand moved toward him, trembling, hovering in the space between them.**

**Nayra saw it. He felt his son's terror. But his mind was already leaping ahead—what did this mean? Should he run to the waystation, report the omen? Would the horn sound?**

**The hand pulled back. The boy did not reach again.**

**The eclipse passed. The lake returned. They walked home in silence. That night the pututu called and Nayra ran his segment twice before dawn, carrying news of the darkened sun. He did not think to ask his son what he had seen.**

Nayra's eyes flew open. His son was bending over him, cloth pressed to his brow, face tight with concern.

Their eyes met.

*I was there,* Nayra wanted to say. *I was always there. But some part of me was forever listening for the horn, forever ready to leave. My father's body never came home, and I learned that men are only partly present—that the rest of them belongs to something else. I taught you to expect nothing, and you learned.*

The words would not come. His throat was raw, his tongue thick. His son wiped the sweat from his temples with the same patient rhythm, giving what he had never received.

Three generations. His father, body lost to empire's war, never made ancestor. Himself, attention lost to empire's service, never fully present. And his son—reaching once in terror, learning not to reach again.

The pattern lay before him now, clear as woven threads. But seeing was not mending.
