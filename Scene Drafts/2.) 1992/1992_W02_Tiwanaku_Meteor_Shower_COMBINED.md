# Tiwanaku Meteor Shower Scene (1992)

The Staff God stared up from a millennium of earth. Dr. Carlos Mamani's brush trembled as he cleared the last soil from the textile's face—serpents gripped in both hands, rays streaming toward three realms. The same pattern his grandmother had tried to teach him to weave before arthritis took her hands.

"Munaycha! Cusicha!" Carlos called. "Come here, please. I want to show you something important."

Five-year-old Cusi dragged her feet as she approached, while thirteen-year-old Maya followed with slightly more interest. Carlos pointed to the textile, its warp-faced stripes still visible despite a millennium in the earth—sequences of three, five, eight, thirteen. Runner's rhythm, his grandmother would have said.

"Listen, our ancestors are speaking to us now in this clothing they made."

Cusi yawned.

Maya ran her fingers along the excavation grid strings, testing their tension like a harp. Always searching for patterns, this one.

Cusi had found a smooth path between the trenches and was running it, back and forth. "I'm carrying messages!" she announced. "Important ones about the sky falling!"

Carlos watched his younger daughter run. The ancient pathways beneath her feet had carried real runners once—chasquis racing between Tiwanaku and distant valleys, knotted strings bouncing against their chests.

---

They followed the ancient road south as darkness gathered—the same route the burial processions would have taken. Cusi ran ahead and doubled back, weaving between them like a shuttle through warp threads.

"Tell us about the comet again," Maya said. She meant 1986—that night in La Paz when Jennifer had whispered that she was pregnant again, and Carlos had felt excitement and terror in equal measure. Three miscarriages since Maya. But under Halley's tail, Cusi had taken hold.

"Any minute now," Jennifer said, checking her watch.

They spread a blanket on flat ground beside the trail. The stars emerged with high-altitude clarity—the same stars the burial procession would have seen, the same constellations the chasquis used to navigate their night runs.

The first meteor drew its line across the dark.

"There!" Cusi pointed.

More followed, building from scattered drops to a steady rain of light. The girls counted in Spanish, English, Quechua—numbers Carlos had taught them, numbers his grandmother had taught him.

Then it happened. Ten brilliant streaks seemed to burst from Perseus simultaneously, spreading across the sky like rays from the Staff God's head.

Out of the corner of his eye, Carlos saw the fiery streaks reflected in water. But there was no water there. Then he heard it—water against stone, the mineral scent of the sacred lake arriving before the image.

He turned. An arm of Lake Titicaca stretched toward him, silver-black under stars. Twenty steps below, a funeral procession wound up from the water's edge, torches reflecting in doubles on the surface. The body on the litter wore the Staff God textile—the same one he'd uncovered this morning.

A young man stood beside the procession route, not mourning but watching the sky. Watching a comet, its tail stretching south toward where the lake would one day recede. The young man's lips moved: *The ancestors are speaking.*

Then Carlos felt himself running—not saw, but *was*—racing along this same path at dawn, carrying news he didn't want to deliver, knotted strings against his chest, the weight of empires in his legs. Somewhere behind him, Spanish boots. The ring of steel. The empire's messages, carried for generations on foot, dying in the throat of the last chasqui.

The lake vanished. The twentieth century reformed around him.

---

"Pachakuti," Carlos whispered.

"You saw something." Jennifer's head was still on his shoulder. "I felt you leave for a moment. Where did you go?"

"I was here. I am always here."

She squeezed his hand. She'd married an archaeologist, but she understood the other part of him—the part his grandmother had nurtured, the part that knew some truths couldn't be published, only carried.

Maya had her notebook out, sketching the meteor trajectories—vertical lines with smaller marks indicating intensity, direction. Her notation looked almost like khipu knots. A thirteen-year-old reinventing an ancient system without knowing it.

Cusi was running again, tracing figure-eights around them. "The stars fell in the same pattern as the textile!" she announced. "Three, five, eight—I counted!"

Carlos pulled his family close. Here were his girls: Maya documenting, Cusi running messages to the stars. Seeds already carrying what fire would try to destroy.

Above them, the last few meteors fell. Below them, a thousand lightfooted runners' had come and gone. That pattern was known until another moment where all three Pachas touched and time folded over itself. In the folding, some patterns are hidden and others revealed in earth, the heavens, and the waters, then in bone, breath and blood. Through two daughters who would carry forward what their father could not yet name.
