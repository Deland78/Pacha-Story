# Tiwanaku Meteor Shower Scene (1992)

The Staff God stared up from a millennium of earth. Dr. Carlos Mamani's brush trembled as he cleared the last soil from the textile's face—serpents gripped in both hands, rays streaming toward three realms. The same iconography from the burial context they'd uncovered that morning. The same pattern his grandmother had tried to teach him to weave before arthritis took her hands.
"Papa, can we go?" Cusi kicked at a pottery shard.
"One moment, mi amor." But his hands wouldn't move. The textile's warp-faced stripes ran in sequences—three, five, eight, thirteen. A fibonacci spiral his academic mind recognized, but his blood remembered differently. Chasqui counts, his grandmother would have said. Runner's rhythm.
Jennifer closed her sketch pad, understanding. "Girls, help me pack the tools."
Maya ran her fingers along the excavation grid strings, testing their tension like a harp. Always searching for patterns, this one. She'd already mapped the entire dig site in her notebook, using a notation system she'd invented—part musical, part mathematical.
Cusi had found a smooth path between the trenches and was running it, back and forth, back and forth. "I'm carrying messages!" she announced. "Important ones about the sky falling!"
Carlos watched his younger daughter run. The ancient pathways beneath her feet had carried real runners once—chasquis racing between Tiwanaku and distant valleys, knotted strings bouncing against their chests, messages encoded in fiber and rhythm.

They followed the ancient road south—the same route the burial procession would have taken, carrying bodies from the city to the ancestral grounds. Carlos had traced it through aerial photos, confirmed it with magnetometry. Now his family walked it as darkness gathered, Cusi still running ahead and doubling back, weaving between them like a shuttle through warp threads.
"Tell us about the comet again," Maya said. She meant 1986—that night in La Paz when he'd proposed to Jennifer under Halley's tail.
"No," Cusi called back. "Tell us about the OLD comet. The one the dead people saw."
Carlos thought of the man laid to rest in 992 CE <CAN'T KNOW THIS DATE>, the Staff God textile his only grave good. The archaeological report would note evidence of crisis: decreased rainfall, abandoned fields. It wouldn't mention what Carlos felt when he lifted the textile—a sensation like running, like carrying urgent news, like time itself folding into knots.
"NASA's estimates Halley's comet would have been visible in 998-89," he said. "Possibly near the time of our burial. The priests would have read it as pachakuti—world-turning."
"Is that why they buried him with the god-face?" Maya asked, pen already moving in her notebook.
"Perhaps. Or perhaps he was leaving a message."
Jennifer checked her watch. "Any minute now."
They spread the blanket on flat ground beside the trail. The stars emerged with high-altitude clarity—the same stars the burial procession would have seen, the same constellations the chasquis used to navigate their night runs.

The first meteor drew its line across the dark.
"There!" Cusi pointed.
More followed, building from scattered drops to a steady rain of light. The girls counted in Spanish, English, Quechua—numbers Carlos had taught them, numbers his grandmother had taught him.
Then it happened. Ten brilliant streaks emerged from Perseus simultaneously, spreading across the sky like fingers of light—or rays from the Staff God's head.
The ground beneath Carlos shifted. Not earthquake. Something else.
Lake Titicaca stretched before him, silver-black under stars, but wrong—too close, lapping at the pyramid's base stones. A funeral procession wound along the water's edge, torches reflecting in doubles on the surface. The body on the litter wore the Staff God textile—no, a different one, older, the pattern Carlos had seen this morning but with subtle variations, an ancestor to the burial shroud.
A young man stood beside the procession route, not mourning but watching the sky. Watching the same meteors? No—a comet, its tail stretching south toward where the lake would one day recede. The young man's lips moved: The ancestors are speaking.
Then Carlos saw himself—or not himself but another runner, centuries between them—racing along this same path at dawn, carrying news of conquistadors in Cajamarca, the Inca taken, the world turning. The runner's eyes held the same expression Carlos felt on his own face: someone who had touched the textile, felt time collapse into knots, understood that all moments existed simultaneously in the weave.
Behind the runner, Spanish boots marched. Swords rang against Inca bronze. The empire's messages, carried for centuries on foot, would soon die in the throat of the last chasqui.
"Papa!" Cusi tugged his hand. "You're crushing my fingers!"
The lake vanished. The twentieth century reformed around him.

"Pachakuti," Carlos whispered.
"You saw it too?" Jennifer asked, though he hadn't described anything.
"The lake. The procession. The runner—" Carlos stopped. How could he explain the runner? That feeling of carrying news across centuries, of being part of a relay that began before Tiwanaku and continued beyond this moment?
Maya had her notebook out, sketching the meteor trajectories. Her notation looked almost like khipu knots—vertical lines with smaller marks indicating intensity, duration, direction. A thirteen-year-old girl reinventing an ancient system without knowing it.
Cusi was running again, tracing figure-eights around them. "The stars fell in the same pattern as the textile!" she announced. "Three, five, eight—I counted!"
Carlos pulled his family close. Tomorrow he would return to the excavation, document everything properly, publish careful academic papers about drought cycles and settlement patterns. But tonight he understood what his grandmother had tried to tell him: some knowledge traveled through blood and breath, through the rhythm of feet on ancient roads, through the grip of serpents in a weaver's design.
"We need to go back to the burial in the morning," he said. "There's something else there. Not another textile—something the runner was carrying."
Above them, the ordinary meteors continued their ancient November shower. Below them, a thousand runners' footfalls had worn the road smooth. And somewhere between—in that place where all three Pachas touched—the Staff God watched with eyes that had seen the comet, would see the conquest, and now witnessed this: a family becoming part of the pattern, another knot in time's vast weaving.
Jennifer squeezed his hand. She'd married an academic, but she understood the other part of him—the part his grandmother had nurtured with stories of chasquis and khipu masters, the part that knew some truths couldn't be published, only carried.
"What will you tell the team?" she asked.
"That we need to expand the excavation. Look for burial goods we might have missed."
"And what will you really be looking for?"
Carlos watched his daughters—Maya documenting the sky with her invented notation, Cusi running messages to the stars.
"The next knot," he said. "The next connection in the pattern."
The meteors continued to fall, each one a thread connecting now to then, earth to sky, the living to those who watch from beyond.
