# W07: The Smallest Threshold (1452 CE)

He had run until the road was no longer stone, but light. 

They found Chuqi on the high trail, just below the pass where the wind screams across the granite teeth of the mountains. He was collapsed on his side, his hands still curled as if clutching a message cord, his chest still and cold. There was no mark on him—no fall, no wound. It was as if his heart had simply decided that the tempo of this world was no longer sufficient. He had pushed his lungs into the Hanan Pacha and had forgotten to pull them back.

The kinetic fire of the runner was out. Only the stone remained.

But we do not let a stone stand alone. 

We gathered in the lakeside ayllu, our voices rising not in individual cries, but in the collective weave of the *qarawis*. It is a sound that does not belong to any one of us; it is the earth itself breathing through our teeth, a heavy, rhythmic pulse that binds our breath to the cold lake and the ancient dust. We did not see a fallen warrior or a broken laborer in the white-clad figure before us. We saw an *angelito*. Though he was sixteen, with the broad shoulders of a chasqui and the scarred calves of a pilgrim, we felt the suddenness of his departure as a communal skipping of a beat. He was our child returning to the stars, and his return was our shared threshold.

The "Wawa Pampay" began at dawn, and we moved as a single body.

We dressed Chuqi in the white linen of the expectant sky. We brought the yellow *retama* and the purple *cantua*, their scents mingling with the lake-salt on our skin. His godfather’s hands, stained with the same earth of the Suka Kollus that sustained us all, prepared the flowered chair. When he lifted it, we all felt the weight.

Our procession moved slowly, a rhythmic swaying that felt like a heartbeat slowed to a crawl. We carried him toward the burial mound, walking the path the Elder had trodden five hundred years before, and where we knew we would eventually follow. 

*Step. Pause. Song. Silence.*

The tempo had shifted from the frantic heat of the run to the cool, steady vibration of the ground beneath our feet. We weren't just walking; we were communal witnesses to the folding of pacha.

His mother walked at our center, her face a mask of obsidian grief that mirrored our own silent anxieties. In her arms, she held Nayra. The baby was quiet, his dark eyes wide and unblinking, fixed on the white-clad figure of his kinsman. He didn't cry. He seemed to absorb the silence we offered him, a silence that held the strength of a lineage.

At the edge of the mound, we stopped. The great-grandmother stepped forward, her voice a reed-broken whisper that rose from the very dust we stood upon.

"He does not go alone," she said, and her words were the echo of our shared heart. "He carries the breath for the one who stays. He is the seed. And the seed must be buried for the field to turn."

We watched as she took the Staff God trinket from Chuqi's cold hand—the same stone he had rubbed for grounding when he ran for us all. When she pressed it into Nayra’s tiny, grasping hand, we felt the connection seal in our own marrow. 

We lowered Chuqi into the earth together, into the Pacha Mama who receives us all and transforms us all. We did not weep with the frantic despair of survivors, for we were not separate. We sang with the steady, shared knowledge of a community that understands that to lose is to plant. The air was cold, the lake was still, and the comet—still a ghost in the white sky—felt like a secret we were all keeping together.

As the last stones were placed, we looked at the baby. Nayra was breathing—a steady, rhythmic pulse that matched the timing of the earth. The kinetic energy hadn't vanished; it had just found a new, smaller threshold within us. We watched the white sky, accepting Chuqi's death not as an end, but as our collective seeding. The raw grief of the *qarawis* settled, transforming into a low, steady vibration of endurance that would outlive the very stones of the road.

The runner was resting. The witness was awake. We were the relay.
